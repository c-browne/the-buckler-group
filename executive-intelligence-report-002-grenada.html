Executive Intelligence Report™ · No. 002

Grenada: From Distinctive Assets to Investment-Ready Growth

Where premium demand, development capital and institutional project preparation are beginning to converge.

Grenada · September 2026Market IntelligenceHospitality · Marine Economy · Capital · Development Readiness
TBG Intelligence Thesis

Grenada's strategic advantage is not simply its tourism brand. It is the emerging intersection of scarce premium assets, increasingly project-specific investment promotion, an unusual development-capital mechanism and demonstrated demand in segments where capacity is already becoming constrained.

Grenada is entering a more consequential stage of its investment cycle. The conventional case—natural beauty, tourism, stability and incentives—does not fully capture what is changing. The more important development is that projects, facilitation, capital formation and operating demand are beginning to connect in ways that can reduce traditional Caribbean execution friction. For sophisticated capital, the question is therefore no longer whether Grenada contains opportunity. It is where opportunity is closest to becoming an executable transaction.

01 · Investment Readiness

The market is shifting from sector promotion to project underwriting.

Grenada's investment-promotion architecture is moving toward a more useful proposition for capital: packaged projects. GIDC has identified that broad statements about tourism, agriculture, health, wellness or energy do not answer the questions sophisticated investors actually underwrite—capital required, prospective return, recovery period, site conditions and the route to execution.

This creates an important distinction between market attractiveness and project investability. Attractive sectors are abundant. Assets with site control, defined development parameters, entitlement visibility, credible sponsorship, preliminary economics and a viable capital pathway are substantially scarcer.

TBG Strategic Implication
Search for execution compression, not simply sector growth.

The strongest near-term advantage may accrue to investors who identify which Grenadian opportunities have already crossed the greatest number of pre-development barriers.

02 · Premium Marine Economy

The clearest scarcity signal is appearing at the top of the yachting market.

Port Louis Marina provides an unusually useful operating-market signal. The 227-berth facility accommodates vessels up to 115 metres. Management reported 53.5% year-over-year growth in yachting visitors and periods in which demand from 80-metre-plus vessels exceeds simultaneous capacity.

This matters because the investable thesis extends well beyond additional berths. Large-yacht demand activates boatyards, technical services, maintenance, provisioning, fuel and bunkering, utilities, transport, food and beverage, waterfront hospitality, longer-term leased berths and specialist professional services.

The sector also introduces a counter-seasonal characteristic. When conventional resorts can be quieter during hurricane season, marina and boatyard activity can remain strong. That potentially diversifies the visitor economy away from a single hospitality demand curve.

TBG Strategic Implication
The visible asset is the yacht. The larger opportunity is the economic system required to service it.

Before underwriting new marina capacity, investors should quantify where expenditure is leaking out of the domestic ecosystem and which high-margin services remain undersupplied.

03 · Infrastructure Behind Luxury

Premium tourism creates infrastructure demand that conventional tourism analysis can miss.

A single 100-metre yacht can impose utility requirements comparable to a small community. Concentrations of large vessels therefore create demand for power, water, wastewater, connectivity, logistics and resilient marine infrastructure at a scale disproportionate to visitor headcount.

This exposes a second-order thesis: distributed energy, renewable systems, water infrastructure, waste treatment, high-capacity connectivity, marine technical services and climate-resilient coastal infrastructure may participate in the growth of Grenada's premium visitor economy without assuming the same operating risk as a hotel or marina owner.

Grenada needs investment in sustainable tourism—but it also needs investment that makes tourism itself sustainable for Grenada.
04 · Development Capital

Investment migration can function as development-capital infrastructure.

Grenada's Investment Migration Agency provides a capital-formation mechanism that can materially alter the conventional development stack for qualifying approved projects. The framework described to TBG allows an approved developer to contribute 20% equity while potentially raising up to 80% of qualifying development requirements through participating investors.

IMA reported an annual flow of approximately 450–550 clients, with roughly 80% selecting approved projects rather than the National Transformation Fund, and a minimum qualifying project investment of US$270,000 per participating investor at the time of TBG's market engagement. The agency also reported close to $2 billion generated for the Government of Grenada over the life of the program, separate from capital raised directly by approved projects.

The mechanism is powerful, but it should not reverse sound underwriting. Investment-migration eligibility cannot substitute for underlying asset quality, durable demand, a credible operator, entitlement clarity or resilient infrastructure.

TBG Underwriting Principle
Asset economics first. Capital mechanism second.

A project that remains commercially defensible before investment-migration capital is introduced has a fundamentally different risk profile from one whose viability depends upon that capital.

05 · Diaspora Capital Arbitrage

Grenadian developers may possess a financing advantage that conventional balance-sheet analysis understates.

The Enhanced Local Developers Program introduces a potentially consequential distinction for qualifying Grenadian developers. IMA indicated that eligible local developers may be able to provide approximately 6% equity while raising up to 94% through the program, compared with the 20% developer-equity requirement described for non-Grenadian approved developers.

If confirmed for a specific sponsor and transaction, the implication is significant: Grenadian-controlled land, diaspora professional expertise, institutional project preparation, investment-migration capital and international operating capability can potentially be assembled into a locally anchored development platform.

The constraint is likely to move from access to capital toward bankability—feasibility, entitlement, sponsor readiness, operator selection, financial modelling and project packaging.

06 · Land-to-Project Conversion

The scarce commodity may not be land. It may be institutionally prepared land.

GIDC has indicated an openness to work with landowners seeking development partners, including potential joint ventures, and to help transform a landholding into a more structured opportunity. This creates a pathway for diaspora and local owners, but also raises the standard for what should be presented to institutional capital.

TBG's development-readiness screen begins with: title and site control; permitted use; planning status; environmental constraints; infrastructure and access; development density; preliminary concept; demand evidence; capital requirement; sponsor capacity; operator strategy; incentive eligibility; and, where relevant, IMA eligibility.

TBG Strategic Implication
A parcel is not an investment product.

Value is created as uncertainty is removed from the route between land, entitlement, capital and operating asset.

07 · Import Substitution

Poultry exposes a wider supply-chain opportunity.

Grenada imports approximately 21 million pounds of chicken annually, while an existing local producer seeking expansion produces approximately 6.1 million pounds. The immediate opportunity is domestic poultry expansion. The more important intelligence signal is the size of the import-substitution question across a tourism-led island economy.

As hospitality, yachting and residential development expand, recurring institutional demand grows for food, energy, logistics, maintenance, technical services and building inputs. The investable question becomes: which imported inputs have sufficient recurring domestic demand to support competitive local or regional production?

That inquiry should extend into controlled-environment agriculture, food processing, cold-chain infrastructure, logistics, renewable energy, hospitality supply and selected building materials.

08 · Blue Economy

Opportunity is broad; readiness is uneven.

The blue economy should not be treated as a single asset class. Marine tourism currently presents observable operating demand. Fisheries value-add is at an earlier stage, with feasibility work examining opportunities beyond basic harvesting and export. Coastal development carries additional environmental and planning requirements, while skills shortages create both an execution constraint and a workforce-development opportunity.

For outside professional-services firms, the strongest entry point is therefore unlikely to be generic market promotion. It is specialized capability tied to a defined readiness gap: feasibility, environmental diligence, marina and waterfront design, project finance, entitlement strategy, operations, technical training or investor packaging.

09 · Entitlement Reality

Grenada offers facilitation—not a single approval counterparty.

Approximately 19 participants can sit within Grenada's wider investment-generation ecosystem, including planning, customs, revenue, permits and other authorities. GIDC describes the resulting model as a virtual one-stop environment: it provides the roadmap, connects investors to relevant institutions and follows approvals through the system.

That is valuable institutional infrastructure, but sophisticated investors should not confuse facilitation with entitlement certainty. Every material consent should still be mapped independently, sequencing dependencies identified, environmental requirements established and realistic approval timelines incorporated into underwriting.

Execution Rule
Engage GIDC early. Underwrite approvals independently.

This is especially important for waterfront, environmentally sensitive and infrastructure-intensive assets.

10 · Climate & Operating Resilience

Resilience belongs inside the financial model.

Grenada's natural environment is both a source of premium positioning and a material development exposure. Waterfront and hospitality underwriting should therefore move resilience from ESG narrative into capital expenditure, operating expenditure and lifecycle planning.

Relevant diligence includes storm exposure, insurance availability and deductibles, backup power, water autonomy, wastewater, corrosion, coastal protection, construction standards, business interruption and replacement reserves. The cheapest project to construct may not be the lowest-cost asset to own.

What the Market May Be Underpricing

Five intelligence advantages

01

Top-end marine scarcity

Capacity pressure among large vessels may create value across services and infrastructure before additional marina supply is delivered.

02

Capital formation as infrastructure

For qualifying projects, investment migration can alter the equity burden and development-financing equation.

03

Local-developer leverage

The ELDP may make certain Grenadian-sponsored projects more financeable than conventional sponsor analysis suggests.

04

Project preparation scarcity

Bankable, entitled and properly structured projects may be scarcer—and more valuable—than raw land or sector concepts.

05

Second-order opportunity

Utilities, marine services, food systems, resilience infrastructure and project preparation can capture growth without identical asset-level risk.

06

A new diaspora market

IMA's emerging engagement with citizens acquired through investment expands the definition of Grenada's diaspora beyond traditional migration markets.

TBG Intelligence Conclusion

Grenada's next advantage will be created in the space between asset and execution.

The jurisdiction does not need a larger inventory of generalized opportunity statements. It needs a deeper inventory of opportunities that capital can actually underwrite. Grenada already possesses many of the ingredients: scarce premium assets, recognizable hospitality demand, marine-economy momentum, an investment-promotion institution prepared to package projects, a capital-formation mechanism capable of supporting qualifying development and an increasingly explicit interest in diaspora participation.

The strategic edge lies in connecting those ingredients before the broader market does: identifying constrained demand, controlling or aligning investable sites, reducing entitlement uncertainty, structuring credible capital stacks and pairing projects with operators and execution partners capable of delivering them.

The opportunity is not simply to invest in Grenada. It is to identify where Grenada is becoming investable before that transition is fully priced by the market.
