Executive Intelligence Report™ · No. 001

Guyana: Hospitality, Real Estate & the Future of Development

Reading the infrastructure, demand and capital signals behind one of the Caribbean Basin's fastest-changing development environments.

Guyana · August 2026Market IntelligenceHospitality · Real Estate · Infrastructure · Development Readiness
TBG Intelligence Thesis

Guyana's investable story is moving beyond resource-led growth. The more consequential opportunity is the repricing of land, hospitality, logistics and urban infrastructure as public investment, international connectivity, private capital and new demand converge faster than the built environment can fully adjust.

Guyana is undergoing a development cycle in which infrastructure can change the economics of a location before conventional market evidence becomes obvious. For sophisticated investors, the advantage lies not in recognizing that the economy is growing, but in identifying which corridors, asset classes and operating platforms will capture that growth as the country's physical and institutional systems catch up with demand.

01 · Infrastructure-Led Repricing

In Guyana, the road can become part of the real-estate thesis.

A development corridor in Houston, Georgetown illustrates the mechanism. A hotel site that initially lacked surrounding roads and services was underwritten against a government infrastructure plan. Within several years, the surrounding highway and development pattern materially changed the location's commercial relevance.

This is a useful signal for investors: in a rapidly building economy, today's peripheral land can become tomorrow's strategic node—but only where public infrastructure, access, utilities and credible private development are sufficiently aligned.

TBG Strategic Implication
Underwrite the infrastructure sequence, not the current streetscape.

The opportunity is to identify corridors where committed infrastructure can compress travel time, unlock land, concentrate demand or create new commercial nodes before those effects are fully reflected in land pricing.

02 · Hospitality Demand

The demand base is broadening beyond a single economic engine.

Guyana reported a 22% increase in visitor arrivals in 2025, with growth across business, trade, leisure, diaspora and nature-oriented travel. Regional Caribbean travel represented roughly one-third of arrivals. Conference and exhibition travel added another demand layer, with more than 15,000 visitors attributed to conferences and exhibitions during the period discussed with TBG.

Hotel supply is responding rapidly. More than 30 hotels were reported under construction nationally, including nine internationally branded properties, while room inventory was expected to rise from approximately 3,280 rooms at the end of 2020 to roughly 5,600 by the end of 2026.

The analytical question is therefore shifting from whether Guyana needs hotel rooms to which hospitality formats, locations and demand segments remain structurally undersupplied after the current pipeline delivers.

TBG Underwriting Question
Will new supply absorb the demand—or expose the next gap?

Investors should segment corporate, government, conference, airport, extended-stay, leisure, eco-lodge and regional demand rather than underwriting Guyana as a single hotel market.

03 · First-Mover Leisure

Luxury eco-hospitality may sit earlier on the development curve than business lodging.

Guyana's business-hospitality pipeline has expanded quickly, but the leisure proposition remains less mature. Public-sector tourism leadership identified a gap in luxury eco-lodges, resorts and all-inclusive product, even as nature tourism and international leisure interest expand.

This creates a different risk-return profile from Georgetown business hotels. The opportunity may carry stronger first-mover characteristics, but it also requires deeper destination creation: air and ground access, experience programming, workforce, utilities, environmental stewardship, operator selection and a credible route to international distribution.

The investable gap is not necessarily another hotel. It may be the hospitality product Guyana's next visitor cannot yet buy.
04 · Bankability

Guyana has begun producing projects that can survive institutional diligence.

One of the strongest signals is not a growth statistic but a financing precedent. The Four Points by Sheraton development combined an international hotel brand, local institutions and multilateral financing. The project underwent environmental, social, governance, technical, insurance and market diligence associated with institutional credit standards.

That matters because frontier and rapidly changing markets are often discounted for perceived execution and financeability risk. A project capable of meeting international brand requirements and multilateral credit standards demonstrates that Guyanese development can be structured to a level recognizable to institutional capital.

TBG Strategic Implication
Bankability itself is becoming a competitive asset.

Sponsors able to present institutional-quality governance, diligence, design, environmental performance and financial reporting should have a different capital-access profile from projects competing principally on land or concept.

05 · Mixed-Use Economics

The hotel can be the demand anchor—not the entire investment.

The Four Points development demonstrates an integrated approach: a 172-room hotel combined with conference capacity and approximately 50,000 square feet of retail and service space. The underlying logic is important. Rapidly expanding business travel can support a broader commercial ecosystem when accommodation, meetings, services and consumption are deliberately aggregated.

This suggests opportunities for mixed-use business ecosystems near airports, major highways, emerging commercial corridors and future urban nodes. The underwriting advantage comes from understanding which uses share demand and which merely add construction cost.

06 · Incentive Architecture

Asset classification changes the economics.

Guyana's incentive framework is not uniform across real estate. For qualifying new hospitality projects, the investment-agreement process described to TBG can provide relief from value-added tax, import duty and, where applicable, excise tax on approved imported construction materials and furnishings. Separate applications may also support tax holidays, subject to applicable approvals and conditions.

By contrast, conventional apartment and condominium development does not receive the same direct tourism-linked tax treatment. Foreign investors were described as able to purchase private land without a mandatory local co-owner, and profit repatriation is permitted under the framework discussed.

TBG Underwriting Principle
Never model “Guyana incentives” generically.

Model the precise asset class, qualifying expenditure, approval pathway and timing. A hotel, serviced accommodation concept, condominium project and mixed-use development can produce materially different incentive outcomes.

07 · Residential Demand

Strong housing demand does not automatically mean subsidized development economics.

Guyana has simultaneously pursued a major homeownership program while experiencing strong private demand for apartments and condominiums, including expatriate demand linked to corporate assignments. More than 50,000 house lots were reported distributed over five years, with a further 40,000 homes contemplated over the subsequent five-year period.

This creates a nuanced residential thesis. Expatriate and executive rental demand may support premium multifamily or condominium economics, but investors should not assume hospitality-style fiscal incentives. The public policy emphasis on homeownership also means product-market fit, tenure strategy and pricing deserve more attention than headline economic growth alone.

08 · Construction & Site Risk

Ground conditions can erase superficial land-price advantages.

Development in parts of Georgetown can require deep piling, land raising, drainage and flood-mitigation work. One hotel development described piles extending roughly 100 feet and raising the site approximately two metres as part of its resilience strategy.

These conditions make geotechnical diligence, drainage, flood elevation, utility capacity and site-preparation costs central to acquisition underwriting. A cheaper parcel can become the more expensive development once the cost of making it buildable is properly recognized.

Execution Rule
Price land after engineering—not before it.

For acquisition screens, TBG would treat geotechnical conditions, finished-floor elevation, drainage and infrastructure connections as components of effective land basis.

09 · Energy & Operating Costs

Power-cost reform could change more than utility bills.

Guyana's gas-to-energy program was presented with an objective of materially reducing electricity costs. If realized at scale, lower and more reliable power costs could affect hotel margins, cold storage, manufacturing, data-intensive businesses, food processing, logistics and other electricity-sensitive operations.

For investors, this should be treated as a scenario variable rather than a guaranteed saving until tariffs, reliability, connection timing and project-specific availability are verified. But it creates a broader question: which asset classes become newly competitive if the country's energy-cost structure materially improves?

10 · Interior Connectivity

The next development map may extend well beyond the coast.

Infrastructure investment is improving access into Regions 7, 8, 9 and other interior markets through roads, bridges, ferries and aviation. Region 9 already has an established tourism circuit, while additional road connectivity toward Brazil has strategic implications beyond tourism.

The investable thesis is not that every interior market is ready today. It is that connectivity can alter the addressable market for eco-hospitality, logistics, agriculture, services and cross-border commerce. Investors should distinguish infrastructure that is announced, tendered, under construction and operational—and then map development timing accordingly.

What the Market May Be Underpricing

Six intelligence advantages

01

Infrastructure-driven land repricing

Committed highways, airport works and new urban nodes can change location economics faster than conventional historical comparables capture.

02

Demand diversification

Business travel is important, but conference, regional, diaspora, leisure and nature markets reduce reliance on a single visitor segment.

03

Institutional bankability

International-brand and multilateral-financed precedents provide a blueprint for sponsors seeking institutional capital.

04

Eco-luxury whitespace

The branded business-hotel pipeline is more visible than the country's still-developing high-end nature and resort proposition.

05

Asset-class incentive asymmetry

Hospitality-linked incentives can materially alter development economics relative to conventional residential real estate.

06

Interior corridor optionality

Improving national and Brazil-facing connectivity may create future nodes for tourism, logistics and commercial development beyond Georgetown.

TBG Intelligence Conclusion

Guyana's advantage is moving from growth to conversion.

Resource wealth created the fiscal capacity and international attention. The next phase depends on converting that momentum into infrastructure, investable land, productive real estate, competitive operating businesses and diversified demand. The market will increasingly reward investors who can distinguish economic growth from project-level economics.

The strongest opportunities are likely to sit where several systems intersect: infrastructure that changes access, demand that is deeper than the existing supply response, an asset class aligned with policy, a site whose engineering risks are understood, a sponsor capable of institutional diligence and a capital structure appropriate to the project.

The opportunity is not simply to participate in Guyana's growth. It is to identify where infrastructure and demand are creating investable geography before the market fully reprices it.
